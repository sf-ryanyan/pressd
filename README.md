## 📍 The real problem

What stops a small shop from selling online is almost never a lack of tools. It is these four things.

**The monthly bill never stops.**
Shopify Basic is $39/month, or $29/month paid annually — $348 to $468 a year. That is before a paid theme, before app subscriptions, and before 2.9% + $0.30 on every card payment. You pay it in a good month and you pay it in a month with no orders at all.

**Your store looks like everyone else's.**
Popular themes are running on tens of thousands of shops. When a customer visits three stores and cannot tell them apart, the only thing left to compare is price.

**One slow second on a phone costs you customers.**
Most theme-based stores load 3–5 MB before anything appears. Your customer is not waiting — they have already swiped away.

**Nobody is looking after the legal pages.**
Returns, privacy, cookies, external-use warnings, California Prop 65. Most small shops either have none of it, or copied a policy from another business that does not apply to theirs.

## 🧩 What was built

PRESSD is a working single-page storefront for a fictional press-on nail brand: six products, a real shopping bag, shipping rules, tax handling and a full set of policies.

Technically it is **one HTML file and a folder of images**. No database, no admin panel, no build step, no server to keep alive.

> The brand and the data are invented, so nothing can actually be ordered.
> What it demonstrates is the **structure** a small shop needs — not a business.

---

## ⭐ What this demonstrates

This is the part the project is really about.

### It is not a theme

The layout follows how high-end beauty and fashion sites actually work — **one product per screen**, wide margins, hairline rules between sections, and no drop-shadowed cards anywhere.

**Fraunces** was chosen for a specific reason: it is a variable font whose strokes thicken automatically as the type gets smaller. That is why both the 46px product name and the 13px spec table stay readable. Most "elegant" display fonts fall apart at small sizes, and that is the single most common way a luxury layout goes wrong.

### The functionality is real

The bag adjusts quantities, calculates line subtotals, switches shipping automatically at a spend threshold, states how tax is handled, and shows a **free-shipping meter** — *"Spend $22.00 more for complimentary shipping."*

It is not a static picture of a shopping cart.

### Legal exposure was considered

External-use warnings, a **California Prop 65 notice**, an FDA disclaimer, and returns and privacy frameworks. Every policy panel states clearly that the wording is illustrative and should be reviewed by a lawyer before the shop takes an order.

A template that tells you to go and see a lawyer is more trustworthy than one that pretends the problem is already handled.

### Accessibility is measured, not claimed

- Every text and interface colour **tested** at 4.5:1 or better — in both light and dark appearance
- Every control reachable by keyboard; dialogs close with Escape
- No horizontal scrolling anywhere from **320px** up to ultrawide
- Animation switches itself off when the operating system asks for reduced motion

### The owner can maintain it

Product names, prices and images all live in **one place** at the top of the file. Change a price once and both the product page and the shopping bag update together — there is no second copy to forget.

Repricing no longer means booking a developer.

## 🔁 Not only nails

The same structure fits any retailer with a **small number of products**: coffee, candles, jewellery, skincare, ceramics, fragrance.
